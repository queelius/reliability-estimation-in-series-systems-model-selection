\begin{abstract}
A reliability engineer modeling a Weibull series system must choose between a full model with a separate shape parameter for each component and a parsimonious reduced model that assumes a common shape. We show that the two questions a practitioner actually cares about, how much accuracy the simplification costs and whether the data can detect the simplification is wrong, are structurally decoupled. The reduced model's prediction bias is a sample-size-independent population quantity that grows as $c_B\,\CV^2$ in the shape coefficient of variation (with $c_B \approx 0.28$ for our system), while the likelihood ratio test's detection power is governed by a noncentrality parameter that scales as $c_D\,n\,\CV^2$ (with $c_D \approx 0.5$), so the detectable shape-CV threshold falls as $\approx 3.5/\sqrt{n}$. We make this precise with a delta-method bias expansion and a Le Cam local-power derivation, and we explain it with a structural result: at any common-shape point the common-shape constraint yields \emph{exactly zero} first-order efficiency gain for system-level prediction. The same identity forces the misspecification bias to be second-order in heterogeneity (no linear term) and explains a counterintuitive finding, that at moderate sample sizes the full model has lower mean time-to-failure mean squared error than the reduced model \emph{even when the common shape is exactly correct}. The practical consequence is a two-regime safety rule: for $n \gtrsim 1000$ the test rejects the reduced model before its bias becomes engineering-significant, and for $n \lesssim 500$ sampling error dominates the small specification bias, so the choice barely matters. All results hold under right-censored and masked failure data satisfying the standard conditions of the masked-data likelihood framework.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Consider a turbine engine whose operation depends on bearings, seals, and blades arranged in series, so that any component failure grounds the engine. Field maintenance logs record the failure time and a short list of suspect components (the candidate set), but exact root-cause analysis requires costly teardown. Warranty programs further right-censor units still in service. The engineer faces a fundamental modeling choice: a full model with separate Weibull shape and scale parameters for each of $m$ components ($2m$ parameters), or a reduced model that assumes a common shape parameter across all components ($m+1$ parameters).

The full model is more flexible but harder to estimate from limited masked data. The reduced model is more parsimonious and, uniquely among single-shape reductions, renders the system lifetime itself Weibull \citep{barlow1975}, enabling closed-form reliability metrics. But is the simplification safe? The bulk of the masked-data literature develops \emph{estimators} for component reliability, by maximum likelihood \citep{Usher-1988, Lin-1993, Joh-1989, Usher-1996}, by Bayesian methods \citep{Lin-1996}, and for distributions beyond the Weibull \citep{Amma-2001, Amma-2004, Zhibi-2005, Zhibi-2007}; standard reliability texts \citep{lawless2003, meeker1998} treat the underlying lifetime models. The model-selection literature asks whether a test can \emph{detect} a difference between candidate models \citep{Craiu-2005, mccool1975}. Neither answers the question a practitioner actually faces: when the reduced model is wrong, does it produce predictions wrong enough to change an engineering decision, and if so, will the data warn us in time?

\subsection{The decoupling}

We show that prediction consequence and statistical detectability are governed by different mechanisms that scale differently in the sample size $n$. Writing $\CV$ for the coefficient of variation of the component shapes, our central findings are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Consequence is sample-size independent and quadratic.} The reduced model's relative bias in system mean time-to-failure (MTTF) is a population quantity, $B \approx c_B\,\CV^2$ with $c_B \approx 0.28$, that does not shrink with $n$. It stays below $1\%$ through $\CV \approx 18\%$.
\item \textbf{Detectability accumulates with $n$.} The likelihood ratio test (LRT) has noncentrality $\approx c_D\,n\,\CV^2$ with $c_D \approx 0.5$, so the smallest detectable $\CV$ falls as $\approx 3.5/\sqrt{n}$.
\end{itemize}
These are not two growth rates of the same quantity; they are two quantities with a shared geometric origin (Section~\ref{sec:theory}). The practical upshot is a two-regime safety rule rather than a single ``safe zone'': at large $n$ the test reliably rejects the reduced model before the bias matters, and at small $n$ the bias is dwarfed by sampling error, so the choice is second order either way.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A decoupling result with calibrated constants.} We derive the $O(\CV^2)$ bias scaling by the delta method \citep{casella2002} and the $c_D\,n\,\CV^2$ detection scaling by Le Cam's local-alternative theory \citep{vandervaart1998}, and we calibrate both constants against simulation. This converts a single simulation study into formulas a practitioner can evaluate at any design point.
\item \textbf{A zero-first-order-gain proposition.} At any common-shape parameter point, the common-shape constraint provides \emph{exactly zero} first-order asymptotic variance reduction for any functional of the system lifetime distribution (MTTF, reliability, quantiles). One identity (Section~\ref{sec:theory}) yields two consequences: the misspecification bias has no linear term (hence $O(\CV^2)$), and the finite-sample MSE ordering is decided at second order, where it favors the \emph{full} model. This refutes the textbook intuition that parsimony reduces variance, for system-level prediction.
\item \textbf{An honest evaluation of pretest model selection.} The natural ``fit both, test, pick one'' procedure is a pretest estimator \citep{bancroft1944, judge1978}. We evaluate it (and its AIC and BIC analogues) and show it exhibits the classical pretest risk hump at $n=100$ but is nearly costless for $n \geq 500$, exactly as the decoupling predicts.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Relation to focused model selection and pretesting}
\label{sec:fic}

The closest framework is the focused information criterion (FIC) of \citet{claeskens2003fic, hjort2003fma}, which selects a model to minimize the estimated asymptotic MSE of a chosen ``focus'' functional, exactly the role MTTF plays here. FIC operates under \emph{local} misspecification, where the parameter departs from the submodel at rate $O(1/\sqrt{n})$, so bias and variance enter at the same order. Our consequence analysis instead treats \emph{fixed} misspecification, whose bias is $O(\CV^2)$ and independent of $n$ (the asymptotics of estimation under a misspecified parametric family go back to \citet{pascual2005} for the Weibull and \citet{White-1982} in general); this is what makes the bias an engineering quantity rather than an asymptotic one. The two views meet in our zero-first-order-gain proposition, which implies that an FIC comparison would favor the full model essentially everywhere, recovering our recommendation by a different route. FIC and its model-averaging relatives \citep{claeskens2008book} have been carried to Cox regression \citep{claeskens2006cox} but not, to our knowledge, to Weibull series systems or masked data; for the broader information-criterion view of model selection see \citet{burnham2002}. The pretest procedure of Section~\ref{sec:adaptive} is the classical pretest estimator \citep{bancroft1944, judge1978, giles1993, danilov2004}, and its small-sample risk hump and the non-uniformity of post-selection inference \citep{leeb2005} are well understood in general; our contribution is to quantify them in the masked Weibull series setting. Tests of common Weibull shape across independent samples date to \citet{mccool1975, mccool1979}; \citet{hart2024} studies estimation under a common-shape Weibull competing-risks model assuming it holds, complementary to our analysis of what happens when it does not.

The paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:framework} sets up the model and data. Section~\ref{sec:consequences} quantifies the consequence of misspecification. Section~\ref{sec:lrt} characterizes detectability. Section~\ref{sec:theory} derives the decoupling and the zero-gain proposition. Section~\ref{sec:adaptive} evaluates pretest selection. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Model Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Series System with Weibull Components}

Consider a series system of $m$ independent components, where the system fails when any component fails. Each component lifetime $T_j$ follows a two-parameter Weibull distribution with shape $k_j > 0$ and scale $\lambda_j > 0$:
\begin{equation}
f_j(t; k_j, \lambda_j) = \frac{k_j}{\lambda_j}\left(\frac{t}{\lambda_j}\right)^{k_j-1} \exp\left\{-\left(\frac{t}{\lambda_j}\right)^{k_j}\right\}, \quad t > 0.
\end{equation}
The system lifetime $T = \min\{T_1, \ldots, T_m\}$ has reliability function
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sys_reliability}
R(t;\v\theta) = \exp\biggl\{-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\biggl(\frac{t}{\lambda_j}\biggr)^{k_j}\biggr\},
\end{equation}
with hazard $h(t;\v\theta) = \sum_j (k_j/\lambda_j)(t/\lambda_j)^{k_j-1}$ and density $f = h \cdot R$, where $\v\theta = (k_1, \lambda_1, \ldots, k_m, \lambda_m)$ is the full parameter vector and $\MTTF_j = \lambda_j \Gamma(1 + 1/k_j)$. The shape governs the failure mode: $k_j>1$ gives an increasing hazard (wear-out), the regime of interest for aging components.

\subsection{Masked and Censored Data}
\label{sec:masked}

We observe $n$ independent systems under two forms of incomplete information. \emph{Right-censoring}: system $i$ has indicator $\delta_i = 1$ if its failure time $t_i$ is observed and $\delta_i = 0$ if it is still operating at the end of observation. \emph{Masking}: for a failed system, instead of the true failed component $K_i$ we observe only a candidate set $C_i \subseteq \{1, \ldots, m\}$ of possible causes. We assume the three standard conditions of the masked-data likelihood framework \citep{towell2025masked}:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(C1)] \textbf{Containment:} the true cause is always in the candidate set, $\Pr(K_i \in C_i) = 1$.
\item[(C2)] \textbf{Symmetry:} conditional on the failure time and on $K_i \in C_i$, the candidate set probability does not depend on which member of $C_i$ is the true cause.
\item[(C3)] \textbf{Independence:} the masking mechanism does not depend on $\v\theta$.
\end{itemize}
Under C1--C3 the masking probabilities factor out of the likelihood and may be dropped, leaving
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loglik}
\ell(\v\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Bigl[ (1-\delta_i)\log R(t_i;\v\theta) + \delta_i\Bigl( \log R(t_i;\v\theta) + \log\!\!\sum_{j \in C_i} h_j(t_i;\v\theta) \Bigr) \Bigr],
\end{equation}
where we used $\sum_{j\in C_i}(h_j/h)\,f = R\sum_{j\in C_i} h_j$. Conditions C1--C3 are the standard assumptions of the masked competing-risks literature \citep{reiser1995, flehinger1998, sen2001}, under which the masked-data likelihood reduces to the candidate-set form above. We maximize \eqref{eq:loglik} numerically with the L-BFGS-B algorithm \citep{byrd1995} using analytic gradients; see \citet{towell2025masked} for the framework and identifiability conditions.

\subsection{The Common-Shape Model}

The \emph{common-shape model} (reduced model) constrains all components to share a single shape $k$, with parameter vector $\v\theta_R = (k, \lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_m)$, reducing the parameter count from $2m$ to $m+1$.

\begin{property}[Weibull Closure {\citep{barlow1975}}]
\label{prop:weibull-closure}
Let $T_1, \ldots, T_m$ be independent with $T_j \sim \text{Weibull}(k, \lambda_j)$. Then $T = \min\{T_1, \ldots, T_m\} \sim \text{Weibull}(k, \lambda_s)$ with $\lambda_s = (\sum_{j=1}^{m} \lambda_j^{-k})^{-1/k}$.
\end{property}

\begin{remark}[Uniqueness]
\label{rem:uniqueness}
Equality of all shapes is not merely sufficient for a Weibull system lifetime; it is necessary. The system cumulative hazard is $\sum_j (t/\lambda_j)^{k_j}$, a sum of power functions $t^{k_j}$. By the linear independence of distinct powers, this equals a single power $(t/\lambda_s)^{k_s}$ for all $t>0$ only if $k_1 = \cdots = k_m = k_s$. Thus the common-shape model is the unique reduction of the full model that preserves the Weibull property of the system, which is what makes it the natural parsimony boundary.
\end{remark}

The practical question is not whether closure holds exactly, but whether the common-shape model's predictions are adequate when shapes are only \emph{approximately} equal. In well-designed systems components age similarly ($k_j \approx k$) but differ in durability ($\lambda_j$), so the common-shape model is physically motivated.

The key estimand is the system MTTF, $\MTTF = \int_0^\infty R(t;\v\theta)\,dt$; we also report the reliability $R(t)$ at $t = \MTTF/2,\ \MTTF,\ 2\,\MTTF$. \subsection{The full model can have lower MSE, even when shapes are equal}
\label{sec:mse}

A counterintuitive finding emerges at $\CV = 0$, where the reduced model is \emph{correctly specified}. Table~\ref{tab:mse} shows that the full model has lower MTTF root-mean-squared error at moderate sample sizes, with the gap statistically significant by a paired test at $n=100$. The mechanism is variance, not bias: at $n=100$ the constrained estimator's MTTF variance is about $20\%$ larger than the unconstrained estimator's, while the squared bias of both is negligible (and what little there is favors the reduced model). The variance inflation shrinks with $n$ and is gone by $n=5000$. This is not a small-sample curiosity to be explained away; it is the finite-$n$ shadow of an exact structural fact, that the common-shape constraint buys \emph{zero} first-order variance reduction for system MTTF, which we prove in Section~\ref{sec:theory}. The traditional argument that parsimony reduces variance holds for the shape parameters themselves but not for the nonlinear system functional built from them.

\section{Detectability: Likelihood Ratio Testing}
\label{sec:lrt}

We test $H_0: k_1 = \cdots = k_m$ with the likelihood ratio statistic
\begin{equation}
\Lambda = -2\bigl(\ell(\hat{\v\theta}_R) - \ell(\hat{\v\theta}_F)\bigr),
\end{equation}
asymptotically $\chi^2_{m-1}$ under $H_0$ \citep{wilks1938}; the null is interior (the common shape is positive), so the standard regularity conditions hold and there is no boundary correction.

\subsection{Type I error and power}

Under perfect homogeneity ($\CV = 0$) the rejection rate ranges from $4.6\%$ to $6.8\%$ across $n$ from $100$ to $10{,}000$. With 500 replications the binomial standard error is about $1\%$, so every value is consistent with the nominal $\alpha = 0.05$; the test is well calibrated. Table~\ref{tab:power-by-cv} reports power as a function of shape $\CV$.

Appendix~\ref{app:data-quality} examines how masking, censoring, and system size affect power: masking has the largest effect ($6\times$ power reduction from $p=0.05$ to $0.70$), then censoring ($2.5\times$), then component count, with no inflation of Type I error. \section{Theoretical Analysis: Why Bias and Detection Decouple}
\label{sec:theory}

The empirical patterns of Sections~\ref{sec:consequences} and \ref{sec:lrt}, quadratic $n$-free bias, $\sqrt{n}$-shrinking detectability, and a full model that wins at $\CV=0$, all follow from a single geometric fact about the common-shape constraint. We state it, prove its consequences, and calibrate the constants.

\subsection{A conjugacy identity}

Work at a common-shape point $\v\theta_0$ with $k_1=\cdots=k_m=k$ and arbitrary scales, and reparameterize each component by $(k_j, \eta_j)$ with $\eta_j = \lambda_j^{-k_j}$, so the system cumulative hazard is $H(t) = \sum_j \eta_j t^{k_j}$, which at the common-shape point is $s\,t^k$ with $s = \sum_j \eta_j$. Let $g$ be any smooth functional of the system lifetime distribution (MTTF, $R(t)$ at a fixed $t$, a quantile), let $I$ be the Fisher information at $\v\theta_0$, and let $V = \{v : \text{the } k\text{-block of } v \text{ is constant across components}\}$ be the reduced model's tangent space embedded in the full $2m$-dimensional space.

\begin{proposition}[Conjugacy identity]
\label{prop:conjugacy}
At any common-shape point, $I^{-1}\nabla g \in V$; equivalently, $\nabla g$ is $I$-conjugate to the shape-contrast directions, $\nabla g^\top I^{-1} A^\top = 0$, where $A$ is the $(m-1)\times 2m$ contrast operator whose rows encode $k_l - k_{l+1} = 0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Two structural facts close the argument in a two-dimensional subspace. (i) \emph{Gradient structure.} Because $g$ depends on $\v\theta$ only through the system law, and at the common-shape point the cumulative hazard is $s\,t^k$, $g$ depends on $\v\eta$ only through the sum $s$; hence $\partial g/\partial \eta_l = c_\eta$ is the same for all $l$, while $\partial H/\partial k_l = \eta_l t^k\log t$ gives $\partial g/\partial k_l = c_k\,\eta_l$. So $\nabla g = (c_\eta\,\v 1,\ c_k\,\v\eta)$ lies in $P := \mathrm{span}\{(\v 1, \v 0), (\v 0, \v\eta)\}$. (ii) \emph{Information structure.} A direct computation of the complete-data score outer products at the common-shape point gives
\[
I_{\eta\eta} = \tfrac1s\,\mathrm{diag}(\v\eta^{-1}),\quad
I_{\eta k} = \tfrac1k(c_1 I_m + \kappa\,\v 1\v\eta^\top),\quad
I_{kk} = \tfrac1{k^2}\bigl(\tfrac{e_2}{s}\mathrm{diag}(\v\eta) + \psi\,\v\eta\v\eta^\top\bigr)
\]
for scalars $c_1,\kappa,e_2,\psi$ depending only on $s$. These four blocks map $U := \mathrm{span}\{(\v\eta, \v 0), (\v 0, \v 1)\}$ into $P$. Since $\dim U = \dim P = 2$ and $I$ is invertible, $I: U \to P$ is a bijection, so $I^{-1}: P \to U$, and as $\nabla g \in P$ we get $I^{-1}\nabla g \in U$, every member of which has a constant $k$-block. The full computation, including the masked, censored case, is in Appendix~\ref{app:proof}.
\end{proof}

Under masking and censoring the gradient structure is unchanged, and the four block proportionalities the proof needs continue to hold; we verify this to Monte Carlo precision for the baseline design ($3\times 10^6$ simulated observations), and the resulting first-order variance gain is below $10^{-5}$ in every configuration tested (Appendix~\ref{app:proof}).

\subsection{Consequence 1: zero efficiency gain and the MSE ordering}

By the theory of constrained maximum likelihood \citep{aitchison1958, silvey1959}, imposing a true constraint changes the asymptotic variance of $g$ from $\mathrm{var}_F = \nabla g^\top I^{-1}\nabla g$ to $\mathrm{var}_R = \mathrm{var}_F - \nabla g^\top I^{-1} A^\top (A I^{-1} A^\top)^{-1} A I^{-1}\nabla g$. The subtracted gain vanishes exactly when $A I^{-1}\nabla g = 0$, which is the conjugacy identity. Hence:

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:zerogain}
Imposing the (true) common-shape constraint yields exactly zero first-order asymptotic variance reduction for any system-level functional, at any scale configuration.
\end{corollary}

So parsimony buys no first-order efficiency for system-level prediction, and the finite-$n$ MSE ordering is decided at second order. The simulations of Section~\ref{sec:mse} show the second-order effect favors the full model: the pooled common-shape error is coherent across components (one shape error shifts all $m$ cumulative hazards the same way), whereas the full model's $m$ shape errors partially diversify inside the nonlinear system functional. This is why $\mathrm{Var}_R > \mathrm{Var}_F$ at $n=100$ and why the gap vanishes as $n$ grows, exactly as Corollary~\ref{cor:zerogain} requires.

\subsection{Consequence 2: the bias has no linear term}

Let the true shapes be $k_l = \bar k + \delta_l$ with $\sum_l \delta_l = 0$, so $\CV \propto \|\v\delta\|$. The reduced model's pseudo-true parameter is the KL projection of the truth onto $V$, and to first order it equals the $I$-orthogonal projection of the parameter shift onto $V$. The MTTF bias $B(\v\delta) = g(\v\theta_R^\ast(\v\delta)) - g(\v\theta_{\text{true}}(\v\delta))$ then has derivative $\partial B/\partial\delta_j = \nabla g\cdot(P_V^I e_{k_j} - e_{k_j})$ at $\v\delta = 0$. The vector $r = P_V^I e_{k_j} - e_{k_j}$ is $I$-orthogonal to $V$, and since $I^{-1}\nabla g \in V$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:conjugacy}, $\nabla g\cdot r = (I^{-1}\nabla g)^\top I r = 0$.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:bias}
The misspecification bias of any system-level functional has no linear term in the shape heterogeneity: $B(\v\delta) = O(\|\v\delta\|^2) = O(\CV^2)$.
\end{corollary}

The same identity that nullifies the variance gain nullifies the linear bias. The deterministic KL projection confirms it: the population bias at $\CV=0$ is $+0.002\%$ (numerically zero), forward and reversed shape perturbations give identical bias (the bias is even in $\v\delta$), and the leading coefficient is $c_B \approx 0.28$ (Figure~\ref{fig:popbias}).

\subsection{Detectability and the decoupling}

The detection side is governed by the same matrix $A I^{-1} A^\top$ but enters differently. Under local alternatives $k_l = \bar k + \delta_l$ with $\|\v\delta\| = O(n^{-1/2})$, the LRT statistic is asymptotically noncentral $\chi^2_{m-1}$ with noncentrality $\mathrm{ncp} = (A\v\delta)^\top (A I^{-1} A^\top)^{-1} (A\v\delta)$ \citep{vandervaart1998}, which scales as $c_D\,n\,\CV^2$. Fitting against the simulations gives $c_D \approx 0.5$ (the per-observation theoretical value is $0.49$), stable across $n \in [10^3, 10^4]$ and $\CV \in [2.7\%, 27\%]$. Figure~\ref{fig:decoupling}(a) shows that plotting power against $n\,\CV^2$ collapses every $(n,\CV)$ condition onto a single noncentral-$\chi^2$ curve, and Figure~\ref{fig:decoupling}(b) shows the resulting detectable threshold falling as $\CV_{50} \approx 3.5/\sqrt{n}$ (fitted log-log slope $-0.47$ versus the theoretical $-0.5$).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{image/decoupling_ncp_collapse.pdf}
\caption{(a) The LRT power for every sample size and heterogeneity level collapses onto one noncentral-$\chi^2_4$ curve when plotted against $n\,\CV^2$, confirming noncentrality $\approx 0.5\,n\,\CV^2$. (b) The detectable shape-CV threshold (50\% power) falls as $\approx 3.5/\sqrt{n}$.}
\label{fig:decoupling}
\end{figure}

The decoupling is now transparent. Bias is a population quantity, $O(\CV^2)$ and free of $n$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:bias}); detectability accumulates with information, $O(n\,\CV^2)$. They share the shape geometry but scale differently in $n$. Two practical regimes follow:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$n \gtrsim 1000$ (detection-led):} the detectable threshold $\CV_{50}\approx 3.5/\sqrt{n}$ sits well below the $\CV\approx 18\%$ bias boundary, so the test rejects the reduced model before its bias becomes engineering-significant.
\item \textbf{$n \lesssim 500$ (noise-dominated):} the test rarely rejects, but the specification bias ($\leq 3\%$ through $\CV\approx 27\%$) is dwarfed by sampling error (RMSE $4$ to $10\%$), so the model choice is second order regardless.
\end{itemize}
At very large $n$ the test may reject even trivially small heterogeneity, but Corollary~\ref{cor:zerogain} shows switching to the full model then costs nothing to first order. This is the structural answer behind the FIC discussion of Section~\ref{sec:fic}: an MSE-focused comparison favors the full model essentially everywhere, while the closed-form bias and power formulas above serve the engineering decision directly at any fixed $n$.

\section{Model Selection in Practice}
\label{sec:adaptive}

Faced with data from an unknown system, the natural procedure is a pretest estimator \citep{bancroft1944, judge1978}: fit both models, compute $\Lambda$, use the reduced model if $\Lambda < \chi^2_{m-1,\,1-\alpha}$ and the full model otherwise. This is not a new method, and its risk properties are governed by the classical pretest theory; our question is how that theory plays out here, given the decoupling.

\subsection{Strategies and results}

We compare always-full, always-reduced, and pretest selection by the LRT, and for completeness the AIC and BIC pretest variants (reduced if $\Lambda$ is below $2(m-1)$ and $(m-1)\log n$, respectively), on MTTF RMSE across $\CV$ from $0$ to $27\%$ at $n \in \{100,500,1000\}$. The LRT pretest tracks the better static strategy. Its RMSE overhead relative to always-full is at most $2.4\%$ at $n=500$ and $n=1000$, but reaches $18\%$ at $n=100$ and $\CV\approx 27\%$: this is the classical pretest risk hump \citep{judge1978}, occurring where the test lacks the power to switch away from the now-biased reduced model. The AIC pretest behaves almost identically (its more liberal threshold helps slightly at small $n$), while the BIC pretest is worst at small $n$ (overhead up to $24\%$): because BIC almost never selects the full model, it behaves like always-reduced and inherits its bias at high $\CV$.

\subsection{Why the pretest is nearly costless at $n \geq 500$}

The decoupling explains the pattern directly. Where the pretest's ``wrong'' choices occur (moderate $\CV$, where the test lacks power) the reduced model's bias is still small by Corollary~\ref{cor:bias}, so misselection is cheap; where the bias is large the test has ample power to switch. The risk hump survives only at $n=100$, where neither regime of the two-regime rule has fully taken hold. Since both fits are already computed to form $\Lambda$, the pretest adds no estimation cost, and at $n \geq 500$ it is within $2.5\%$ of always knowing the right model.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked when a reliability engineer can safely simplify a Weibull series model to a common shape, and answered it structurally. Misspecification consequence and statistical detectability are decoupled: the reduced model's prediction bias is a sample-size-independent population quantity, $O(\CV^2)$ with coefficient $\approx 0.28$, while the likelihood ratio test's detectability scales as $\approx 0.5\,n\,\CV^2$, so the detectable threshold falls as $3.5/\sqrt{n}$. A single conjugacy identity drives both halves: at any common-shape point the constraint is invisible to first order for system-level prediction, which forces the bias to be second-order in heterogeneity and shows that the common-shape constraint provides zero first-order variance gain. The latter explains a finding that contradicts the parsimony-reduces-variance intuition: at moderate sample sizes the full model has lower MTTF MSE than the reduced model even when the common shape is exactly correct.

For practice this yields a two-regime safety rule. For $n \gtrsim 1000$ the LRT rejects the reduced model before its bias becomes engineering-significant; for $n \lesssim 500$ the specification bias is dominated by sampling error, so the choice is second order. The natural pretest procedure inherits this: it is within $2.5\%$ of the better static strategy for $n \geq 500$, with a residual risk hump at $n = 100$ that is the classical pretest phenomenon, not a defect of the masked-data setting. The case for the common-shape model rests on Weibull closure and engineering-adequate predictions, never on statistical efficiency.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Our calibrated constants ($c_B \approx 0.28$, $c_D \approx 0.5$, $\CV_{50}\approx 3.5/\sqrt{n}$) are derived for the baseline 5-component system with equally spaced shapes; the scaling laws ($O(\CV^2)$, $O(n\CV^2)$) and the zero-gain proposition hold generally, but the coefficients should be recomputed for a given system from the formulas of Section~\ref{sec:theory}. Real systems may exhibit clustered or asymmetric heterogeneity rather than the symmetric spread studied here. The masked-case proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:conjugacy} is reduced to four block proportionalities verified numerically; a fully analytic masked-data proof is left open.

\paragraph{Future directions.} Extending the decoupling to non-Weibull families (where closure may fail), to $k$-out-of-$m$ systems, and to the focused-information-criterion machinery under fixed rather than local misspecification would broaden the reach of the structural result.

\section{Simulation Details}
\label{app:sim}

All fits use L-BFGS-B \citep{byrd1995} with analytic gradients. Full-model fits use \texttt{parscale} equal to the parameter vector; reduced-model fits add a Nelder--Mead polishing step from the L-BFGS-B solution because the common-shape likelihood surface is mildly multimodal at high heterogeneity. Non-convergent full-model fits and the rare replications with $\Lambda < 0$ (an optimizer-failure indicator) are excluded: fewer than $3/500$ per condition near $\CV = 0$, rising to about $13/500$ at $\CV \approx 41\%$, so the headline results (which live at $\CV \leq 27\%$) are essentially unaffected. The population specification bias of Table~\ref{tab:consequence} and Figure~\ref{fig:popbias} is computed without simulation, by minimizing the expected reduced-model negative log-likelihood (a deterministic integral under the true masking and censoring law) over the reduced parameters and evaluating the resulting MTTF; this gives the exact $n\to\infty$ pseudo-true bias and isolates specification from estimation error.

\section{Proof of the Conjugacy Identity}
\label{app:proof}

\paragraph{Complete data.} At the common-shape point, with $W = T^k \sim \mathrm{Exponential}(s)$ and failed component $J$ independent of $W$ with $\Pr(J=l) = \eta_l/s$, the complete-data scores are $\partial \ell/\partial \eta_l = \mathbf{1}\{l = J\}/\eta_l - t^k$ and $\partial \ell/\partial k_l = k^{-1}[(1 + \log W)\mathbf{1}\{l=J\} - \eta_l W \log W]$. Taking expectations of the score outer products gives the blocks quoted in the proof sketch of Section~\ref{sec:theory}, with $c_1 = (1 + \mathbb{E}\log W)/s$, $\kappa = \mathbb{E}[W^2\log W] - (2\mathbb{E}[W\log W] + \mathbb{E}[W])/s$, $e_2 = \mathbb{E}[(1+\log W)^2]$, and $\psi = \mathbb{E}[W^2(\log W)^2] - 2\mathbb{E}[(1+\log W)W\log W]/s$. Writing $\nabla g = (c_\eta \v 1, c_k \v\eta)$ and seeking $v = (b\v\eta, a\v 1) \in U$ with $Iv = \nabla g$ reduces, using $\v\eta^\top\v 1 = s$, to a $2\times 2$ linear system in $(a,b)$ that is solvable whenever the scalar $D = (e_2/s + \psi s) - s(c_1 + \kappa s)^2$ is nonzero (equivalently, $I$ is nonsingular on the relevant subspace). The unique solution has constant $k$-block $a\v 1$, proving $I^{-1}\nabla g \in V$.

\paragraph{Masked and censored data.} The gradient structure $\nabla g = (c_\eta\v 1, c_k\v\eta)$ is unchanged, since $g$ remains a functional of the system law. The proof needs only that $I$ map $U$ into $P$, i.e.\ the four proportionalities $I_{\eta\eta}\v\eta \propto \v 1$, $I_{k\eta}\v\eta \propto \v\eta$, $I_{\eta k}\v 1 \propto \v 1$, $I_{kk}\v 1 \propto \v\eta$. We verified these for the baseline design ($p = 0.215$, $q = 0.825$, $m=5$) by Monte Carlo, computing $I$ from $3\times 10^6$ simulated masked, censored observations: the relative deviations from proportionality are at the $5\times 10^{-4}$ Monte Carlo floor, and the end-to-end first-order variance gain for MTTF and $R(t)$ is below $10^{-5}$. A fully analytic treatment requires propagating the candidate-set averaging through the block expectations and is left open.
